Mechanical Design · FEA · AI/ML

Physics-Informed Gaussian Process Modeling & Bayesian Optimization of a Robotic Gripper Mechanism

Three-arm, lead-screw-driven gripper for UAV capture of flexible fishnets, structurally optimized with Gaussian-process Bayesian optimization starting from only five finite element simulations.

  • SolidWorks
  • ANSYS
  • Python
  • GP / BO
  • 3D printing

Engineering problem

Fishnets deform during grasping and give irregular contact, so the gripper (central body, three arms, pivot shafts and a self-locking lead screw) must hold a 100 N per-arm design load. Changing one dimension shifts stress between the shaft, arms and body, and every finite element run is expensive — the design has to be optimized with very few simulations.

Methodology

  • Four design variables: arm length, shaft radius, arm thickness and shaft length; objective = worst-case (max of shaft, arm, body) von Mises stress.
  • ANSYS static structural model with a mesh-convergence study to fix a consistent 2.0 mm mesh protocol.
  • Only 5 shared initial FE simulations (Latin hypercube), then batches of 5 (3 exploitation + 2 exploration) per round.
  • Four surrogates compared under identical acquisition: direct GP, composite GP (one per component), physics-feature GP (dimensionless ratios) and a physics-informed composite GP (closed-form stress templates × GP residuals).
  • Worst-case stress distribution by Monte Carlo propagation through the max operator.
  • Accuracy tracked without held-out data: leave-one-out CV, prospective prediction of every new batch, and cross-surrogate tests.
  • 3D-printed prototype with a motor-control circuit, tested on a fishnet.

Key results

  • All four surrogates beat the best initial design (15.73 MPa) in the first round and finished at 14.82–14.90 MPa — a 5.2–5.8% reduction in worst-case stress.
  • The physics-informed and physics-feature GPs came within 1% of the best design after 10 FE evaluations, versus 15 for the composite GP and 20 for the direct GP.
  • Best design has a safety factor of 16.9 against yield at the 100 N per-arm load.
  • Five-coefficient physics templates explained ~97% of the log-stress variance; final surrogates predicted unseen designs with 0.9–2.4% mean error.
  • Honest limit: the surrogates' best designs differ by only 0.22–0.58%, within local FE variability (up to 3%), so convergence speed — not final stress — is the meaningful comparison.

AI / Machine Learning · Energy

Multi-Station Electric Vehicle Charging Demand Forecasting

Day-ahead charging-demand forecasting at five public stations, comparing six ML and deep-learning models under three training strategies, plus solar PV and battery integration.

  • Python
  • GRU / LSTM
  • XGBoost
  • Bayesian optimization

Engineering problem

Station-level demand is noisy and histories are short. Which models and training strategies work best when only a few years of data from a few stations exist — and how does the forecast demand compare with on-site solar generation?

Methodology

  • Five public stations in Palo Alto, California, 2012–2016: 1,373 days (6,865 station-days; 0.89% missing values interpolated).
  • Random Forest, XGBoost, LSTM, GRU, Transformer and CNN–LSTM.
  • Individual, Global and Cluster-Based learning.
  • Every model tuned by Bayesian optimization with equal budgets; three benchmarks, five error metrics and corrected significance tests.
  • Rooftop PV estimated from measured irradiance; battery size chosen by Bayesian optimization.

Key results

  • GRU with Global learning had the lowest mean absolute error (10.61 kWh), significantly lower than every other model.
  • All models cut the persistence (naive) error by about one third.
  • Honest finding: the models differed by only 0.34 kWh, beat a constant training-mean forecast by only 12–15%, and tracked typical demand rather than peaks.
  • Global learning was most accurate for five of six models, but no framework difference was statistically significant with five stations.
  • Rooftop PV covered 84.4% of total demand (24–212% in individual months); optimized battery sizing brought station operating cost to zero or below.

Enable a small car-like robot to build a map of an unknown indoor space and navigate autonomously to goal poses while avoiding obstacles — with the turning constraints of Ackermann steering.

Robotics · Motion planning

Industrial Robotic Arm Programming & Motion Planning — UR10e and ABB IRB 120T

Kinematics, workspace analysis and collision-free pick-and-place planning for a planar RRR arm, a UR10e and an ABB IRB 120T in MATLAB.

  • MATLAB
  • Robotics System Toolbox
  • UR10e
  • ABB IRB 120T

Engineering problem

Machine tending: a UR10e must carry a part from an infeed conveyor into an inspection fixture, but a 0.3 m guard wall blocks the straight path. The gripper must stay vertical and the arm must never touch the wall.

Methodology

  • RRR arm: rigid-body tree, workspace from 20,000 sampled configurations, forward kinematics checked by hand, closed-form and numerical inverse kinematics (elbow-up), cubic joint trajectories through four waypoints.
  • UR10e: eight waypoints (vertical approach, lift, high transfer, vertical descent), warm-started inverse kinematics, cubic joint trajectory (7 s, 0.05 s steps).
  • Collision check of the full robot geometry against the wall at every time step.
  • ABB IRB 120T: joint-space animation and end-effector trajectory to a goal pose.

Key results

  • UR10e: no collision at any of the 141 time samples; minimum clearance 0.089 m, with 0.3 m clearance as the gripper crosses the wall.
  • Joint 5 held at −90° throughout, keeping the gripper vertical; all joint trajectories smooth with zero velocity at waypoints.
  • RRR: workspace is a full 0.9 m disk; forward kinematics (0.6022, 0.6105) m matched the hand calculation; closed-form and numerical IK agreed.

Robotics · Path planning

Warehouse Mobile-Robot Path Planning — PRM vs. RRT

Sampling-based planning for a delivery robot on a 60 × 60 m warehouse map: start → pick-up → packing room, including replanning when the goal changes mid-route.

  • MATLAB
  • PRM
  • RRT
  • Occupancy maps

Engineering problem

Plan collision-free routes through a cluttered warehouse under node budgets, and react when the drop-off goal changes while the robot is already moving.

Methodology

  • Binary occupancy map, 1 cell per metre.
  • PRM: 500 nodes (limit 800), 10 m connection distance, re-sampled until both legs connect.
  • RRT: SE(2) state space, max connection distance 0.9 m, goal bias 0.2, motions validated every 0.1 m, total budget under 3,000 nodes.
  • Replanning: new RRT tree grown from the robot's mid-route state to the new goal.

Key results

  • RRT used 1,753 nodes in total (leg 1: 1,295 nodes, 139.7 m; leg 2: 458 nodes, 96.8 m) — within the 3,000-node budget.
  • Mid-route replanning needed a 435-node tree (2,188 nodes overall).

Mechanical Design · Modal FEA

Structural Design and FEA of a Vibration Test Fixture

Aluminum 6061-T6 fixture for a black-box unit, redesigned over five ANSYS modal iterations to push the first natural frequency from 652 Hz to 1,326 Hz while staying under 50 lb.

  • SolidWorks
  • ANSYS Modal
  • Al 6061-T6

Engineering problem

A fixture must be far stiffer than the unit it holds so that sine and random tests (5–1000 Hz) measure the unit, not the fixture. Requirements:

  • First natural frequency > 1,200 Hz
  • Length 10–12 in, thickness ≤ 1.5 in
  • Mass ≤ 50 lb, 0.25 in bore holes

Methodology

  • SolidWorks CAD; ANSYS modal analysis (first six modes) with bolted base boundary conditions.
  • Five design iterations driven by where modal displacement concentrated: side curves, fins, a half-circular cutout, and removal of mass from high-displacement regions.
  • Final: thinner, extended base; thickness raised to the 1.5 in limit; dedicated support at the top-middle hot spot.

Key results

  • First natural frequency raised from 652.5 Hz (iteration 1) to 1,325.7 Hz — 10% above the 1,200 Hz requirement.
  • Final mass 46.46 lb, meeting every dimensional and weight constraint.
  • Lesson: adding material indiscriminately (iteration 3) increased mass without fixing the low mode; targeted stiffening at the deformation hot spot did.

Autonomous Robotic Vacuum Cleaner

Arduino-controlled vacuum robot with a custom semi-closed impeller, ultrasonic obstacle detection and a material-optimized CAD body.

  • Arduino
  • Ultrasonic sensors
  • SolidWorks
  • Impeller design

Engineering problem

Build a low-cost robot that cleans autonomously: it needs enough suction, a light but strong body, cooling for the electronics, and navigation that copes with both static and moving obstacles.

Methodology

  • CAD body optimized to minimize material while keeping structural integrity, with thermal management for the electronics.
  • Custom semi-closed impeller and fan housing designed in SolidWorks.
  • Ultrasonic sensors around the body for near-360° awareness; Arduino Uno and Nano control.
  • Navigation logic: go forward when clear; turn toward the clearer side; wait for moving objects to pass; back out when there is no way forward.
  • Motor speeds tuned during forward motion to stop tilting and keep a straight path.

Key results

  • Working autonomous navigation demonstrated (video below).
  • Suction measured with a U-tube manometer: 15 mm H₂O (≈ 1.45 × 10⁻³ atm). This is only a partial reading — the 10 mm manometer inlet on a 20 mm suction pipe under-reads, so true suction is expected to be significantly higher.

Engineering problem

Automatically detect defective parts on a moving production line and physically separate them from good parts without manual inspection.

Methodology

Augmented Reality-Based Parking Safety Application

Unity + AR Foundation mobile prototype that places a virtual parking scene on real surfaces and warns the driver near boundaries — rated 4.29 / 5 for usability by eight users.

  • Unity
  • AR Foundation
  • Usability study

Engineering problem

New drivers struggle to judge the space between a car and parking boundaries. Can a simple AR app make boundaries and hazards visible and teach safer parking?

Methodology

  • Plane detection anchors the scene; tap-to-place spawns the parking layout and car.
  • On-screen forward/back/left/right control of the virtual car.
  • Trigger zones show a warning when the car nears a boundary.
  • Usability survey: 8 participants, 7 items on a 5-point Likert scale, analyzed in Python.

Key results

  • Mean usability score 4.29 / 5; boundary awareness rated highest at 4.75.
  • Ease of use, boundary awareness and overall usability received 100% agreement.
  • Limits: small sample, demo-based evaluation, and no measurement of actual parking accuracy or comparison with a 2D control.
